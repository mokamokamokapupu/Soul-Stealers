soul studies · a passion project

What do we mean, when we say soul?

I've spent the better part of a year collecting definitions, and every tradition hands me a different one. This site is my attempt to lay them side by side and see what actually survives the comparison.

Everything I keep noticing about how people work.

The word "soul" gets all the attention, but the machinery around it turned out to be just as strange: attention, identity, influence, belief, and the stories groups tell about themselves. These are the seven topics I cannot stop reading about, each with something small to click on.

Psychology

THE INDIVIDUAL

This is where I started, mostly because it was the one domain I could test on myself. Perception, memory, motivation, identity — none of them behave like the fixed traits I assumed they were. They're processes, running constantly, whether or not you ever audit them.

Cognitive biases

DECISIONS

Biases get dismissed as "bad thinking," which I think misses the point. Most of them persist because a fast, mostly-right answer beats a slow, perfect one under uncertainty. The trouble starts when the shortcut fires in a situation it was never built for.

05

Manipulation & coercion

BOUNDARIES

Influence crosses a line when it stops respecting your ability to choose. Coercion is the obvious version — pressure, threats, penalties for refusing. Manipulation is the subtler one: hiding context, leaning on a vulnerability, engineering a decision while letting you believe it was entirely yours.

Transparent influenceThe reasons are visible, and you can disagree without being punished for it.
Manipulative influenceSomething important is hidden or distorted, specifically to steer your choice.
Coercive influenceRefusal carries a threat — a penalty, a loss, a door that closes on you.

07

Social dynamics

NETWORKS

Groups grow status ladders, coalitions, in-jokes, unwritten rules, and origin stories — usually without anyone deciding to build any of it. Tiny interactions compound into structures that outlast the people who started them.

It starts with contact: people notice each other, size up safety and relevance, and form first impressions that are annoyingly durable.

Case file · 01

The power of the room

Picture three people who each privately disagree with a group decision. The discussion opens, and each of them watches the other two stay quiet. The silence becomes evidence: "maybe I'm the only one who sees a problem." I have watched this exact thing happen in a student council meeting, which is partly why I'm writing any of this.

Change one variable — let a single person say the quiet part out loud — and the social information available to everyone in the room updates at once. That asymmetry, where one voice can reprice the whole conversation, still gets me.

Field notes · 03

Questions I keep in my notes app

What changes when nobody is watching?

Private behavior is a decent litmus test for which of your motives depend on an audience and which ones you'd maintain alone in a room.

When does a norm become invisible?

Repeat a behavior long enough and it stops registering as a choice at all. It just becomes "how things are," which is exactly when it's worth re-examining.

Can a belief be useful and inaccurate?

Apparently yes. A simplified model can guide action perfectly well while failing to describe the world — my entire mental map of my own city works like this.

Where does agency actually sit?

I've stopped treating it as all-or-nothing. It reads more like a relationship between choices, constraints, knowledge, and habit — which is less satisfying but probably more honest.

THE LONG QUESTION SOUL · SELF · SOCIETY

Say the word out loud and it means something different depending on who's listening. To my grandmother it's a theological claim: an immortal, immaterial thing that outlives the body. To my playlist it's a genre. Between those two poles sits most of recorded philosophy, circling one stubborn question that refuses to die of old age: what is it, in a person, that makes them them?

Greek philosophy

Psyche — the thing that animates

The Greek psyché didn't start out ghostly at all — it meant breath, the animating difference between a living body and a corpse. Plato took it much further: the soul was immortal, split into reason, spirit, and appetite, and only temporarily stuck in flesh. Aristotle's response reads to me like the first great "well, actually" in Western philosophy — the soul isn't a separate substance riding around in a body, it's the body's form, the organizing pattern that makes a living thing alive. No more separable from it than the shape of a fist is separable from the hand making it.

Dualism

Descartes and the ghost in the machine

Centuries later, Descartes reopened the split with his divide between res cogitans and res extensa — the thinking thing and the extended thing, mind and matter as two fundamentally different kinds of stuff. The soul becomes the seat of thought and identity, interfacing with a mechanical body through (he guessed, wrongly) the pineal gland. Critics have now spent four hundred years asking the follow-up he never satisfyingly answered, and honestly it was the first question I had too: if mind and matter are made of entirely different stuff, how does one push the other around?

Abrahamic traditions

A soul that is judged

In Jewish, Christian, and Islamic thought, the soul carries moral weight — it's the part of a person accountable before God, the thing that persists to face judgment after the body fails. The details diverge more than I expected going in: whether the soul is created at conception or breathed in later, whether it sleeps until resurrection or proceeds directly to an afterlife, whether it's immortal by nature or only by divine gift. But the throughline holds steady across all three — the soul is where moral responsibility lives.

Dharmic traditions

Atman, and the tradition that denies it

Hindu philosophy generally holds that beneath the changing self sits an atman — an unchanging essence that is ultimately identical with the ground of reality itself, Brahman. Buddhism, growing out of the same soil, planted its flag on the exact opposite claim: anatta, non-self. What we call a soul, in the Buddhist analysis, is a fast-moving bundle of sensations, perceptions, and mental formations, mistaken for something solid only because it's persistent — the way a river looks like a single object from a distance. Two traditions sharing a vocabulary of liberation, arriving at opposite answers about whether anyone is home to be liberated. This is still my favorite disagreement in the whole project.

After the soul

What secular thought kept, and what it dropped

Modern psychology mostly retired the word, swapping in "self," "mind," "identity," "consciousness" — terms it hoped could do the same work without the metaphysical debt. Whether the swap actually succeeded is still argued. Neuroscience can show you which regions light up when someone recalls a memory or feels regret, but that hasn't dissolved the older question so much as relocated it: is a person fully described by that map, or does something escape it? What convinces me the question isn't finished: people who would never call themselves religious still reach for "soul" when nothing else fits — soul food, soul music, an old friend described as an old soul. The word outlived the doctrine.

So here's where I've landed, at least this semester: not on a settled fact, but on a question every tradition took seriously enough to keep asking, each landing somewhere different, none able to fully let it go. What survives across all of them is the intuition that a person is not merely their measurements — and that the remainder, whatever it turns out to be, deserves a name.
