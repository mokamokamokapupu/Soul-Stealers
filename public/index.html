SOUL STUDIES RESEARCH INITIATIVE · MONOGRAPH IV

The Soul as Explanatory Construct

A Comparative Survey of Personhood Across Philosophical, Theological, and Cognitive-Scientific Traditions

Independent research monograph Second revision · August 2026 Open access Not peer reviewed
personhood psychē substance dualism moral agency ātman / anattā self-model theory social ontology

Abstract

Few terms in the history of ideas have carried as much explanatory weight, across as many otherwise incompatible frameworks, as soul. The present monograph surveys the concept's principal formulations — Homeric and classical Greek psychology, Cartesian substance dualism, the moral-forensic soul of the Abrahamic theologies, the Dharmic debate between essentialist and non-self analyses, and the successor vocabulary of the modern mind sciences — and asks a single organizing question: what work has the concept been asked to do, and which of those tasks remain unassigned after its formal retirement? We argue that "soul" has historically bundled four distinguishable functions: an animation function (what separates the living from the dead), an identity function (what makes a person the same person over time), a moral function (what bears responsibility and desert), and a remainder function (what resists exhaustive physical description). Successor concepts in psychology and neuroscience have inherited these tasks unevenly, and the persistence of soul-language in ostensibly secular registers is best explained by the remainder function's continued vacancy.

SCOPE & METHOD

This survey is comparative and conceptual rather than doxographic: sources are read for the function a soul-concept performs within its home framework, not catalogued for completeness. Primary texts are cited in standard editions; translations follow the conventions noted in the references. No claim of confessional or metaphysical adjudication is made at any point — where traditions disagree, the disagreement itself is treated as the datum of interest.

Structure of the monograph

Sections I–IV reconstruct the four historically dominant families of soul-concepts in roughly chronological order. Section V examines the vocabulary that replaced them in the empirical mind sciences and evaluates the completeness of that replacement. Section VI turns to the social and legal registers in which personhood is conferred rather than discovered. Section VII synthesizes the findings under the four-function analysis proposed above.

Working definitions adopted throughout

Soul-concept: any theoretical posit whose primary role is to account for one or more of the four functions named in the abstract. Substance: an entity capable of independent existence, per the Cartesian usage. Form: the organizational principle of a body, per the Aristotelian usage. Person: the bearer of identity and accountability, deliberately left neutral between metaphysical accounts. These definitions are stipulative; nothing in the argument depends on their being the only admissible ones.

Limitations of the survey

Three limitations deserve notice. First, the survey privileges textual traditions with continuous commentarial histories, under-representing oral and indigenous ontologies whose soul-concepts are frequently plural and relational. Second, the treatment of the mind sciences compresses a century of experimental work into its conceptual upshot. Third, comparative work of this kind inevitably flattens internal diversity — "Christian views of the soul" names a family of positions, not a position.

A note on terminology and transliteration

Greek terms are transliterated without diacritics except where a distinction depends on them (psychē, thymos, nous). Sanskrit and Pāli terms are given in simplified IAST (ātman, anattā, skandha). Hebrew and Arabic terms follow common scholarly usage (nefesh, ruaḥ, nafs, rūḥ). Where a single English gloss would prejudge an interpretive question, the source term is retained.

I · Antiquity: from breath to tripartite structure

The earliest Greek usage of psychē is strikingly austere. In the Homeric poems it is not the seat of personality, emotion, or thought — those roles are distributed across thymos and nous — but the bare animating difference between a living body and a corpse, exhaled at death and persisting in Hades as an attenuated image. The concept's later career is a history of consolidation: functions originally scattered across several terms were progressively gathered into psychē alone, until by the classical period it named the whole inner life of the person.

Plato's Republic completes this consolidation and adds internal structure: the soul comprises a reasoning part (logistikon), a spirited part (thymoeides), and an appetitive part (epithymētikon), with justice defined as their correct hierarchical ordering. In the Phaedo, the soul's kinship with the unchanging Forms grounds the argument for its immortality — the first fully explicit claim, in the Greek tradition, that the identity function and the animation function are performed by something separable from the body.

KEY POSITION · HYLOMORPHISM

Aristotle's De Anima rejects the separability thesis at its root. The soul is the form of a natural body having life potentially within it — not a substance residing in the body but the body's own organization, actuality, and capacity-profile. On this analysis, asking whether the soul survives the body is a category error, "as if one were to ask whether the wax and the shape given to it by the stamp are one." Hylomorphism thereby retains the animation and identity functions while discharging the metaphysical debt of dualism — a solution modern functionalists have repeatedly rediscovered.

The Homeric inventory of soul-words

Homeric psychology distributes across at least three terms what later Greek gathers into one: psychē (life-breath, shade), thymos (the surge of emotion and impulse, located in the chest), and nous (the faculty of seeing a situation for what it is). None of these is a bearer of moral responsibility in the later sense; Homeric agents are praised and blamed for deeds, not for the states of an inner substance. The consolidation of these terms is itself evidence that "the soul" is a constructed explanatory object, assembled to answer questions its components could not answer separately.

Stoic pneuma: a corporeal soul

The Stoics accepted that the soul performs the animation and identity functions while denying that anything incorporeal exists. Their solution — the soul as pneuma, a fine-grained corporeal breath pervading the body in graded tensions — demonstrates that antiquity already contained a physicalist option. That this option was available yet repeatedly declined by the majority traditions suggests the persistence of dualism is not explained by a mere absence of alternatives.

Pythagorean and Orphic transmigration

Pre-Platonic transmigration doctrines are the earliest Greek formulations in which the identity function is performed across multiple embodiments. Their significance for the present survey is structural: once the soul is what persists between bodies, the body is demoted from constituent of the person to temporary vehicle — the exact inversion of the Homeric picture, achieved within three centuries.

"If the eye were an animal, sight would be its soul." — Aristotle, De Anima II.1 (paraphrase of the standard example)

II · Substance dualism and the early-modern settlement

Descartes' Meditations (1641) reorganized the entire problem-space. Where ancient and scholastic treatments had graded soul-functions across plants, animals, and humans, Descartes drew a single line: reality divides into res cogitans (thinking substance, unextended, indivisible) and res extensa (extended substance, mechanical, divisible). Animals fell wholly on the extended side; the human being became a uniquely problematic composite. The soul was now coextensive with the mind, and the identity and remainder functions were concentrated in a substance defined precisely by its exclusion from physical description.

The settlement purchased clarity at the price of an interface problem that has never been discharged. If mind and body share no properties, the causal traffic between them — volition moving limbs, injury producing pain — becomes exactly as mysterious as the data it was meant to explain. Descartes' own proposal, that the interaction is transacted at the pineal gland, relocated the mystery without reducing it, as his sharpest correspondent saw immediately.

THE STANDING OBJECTION

Princess Elisabeth of Bohemia, in correspondence with Descartes (May 1643), posed the question that still organizes philosophy of mind: how can an unextended thing determine the motion of an extended thing, when determination of motion appears to require contact, and contact requires extension? Descartes' reply — that the union of mind and body is a primitive notion, intelligible in daily life but obscured by philosophy — concedes the systematic point while declining the systematic burden. Every subsequent dualism inherits Elisabeth's question.

Occasionalism and pre-established harmony

The generation after Descartes treated the interaction problem as decisive and rebuilt around it. Malebranche's occasionalism assigns all genuine causation to God, mental and physical events being merely occasions of divine action; Leibniz's pre-established harmony denies interaction altogether, mind and body running as synchronized clocks wound at creation. That the era's finest systematic minds preferred these costs to Elisabeth's problem measures the problem's perceived severity.

Property dualism as the modern residue

Contemporary property dualism retains one substance but two property-families, holding that phenomenal properties fail to reduce to physical ones. It is best read as the Cartesian remainder function surviving the death of Cartesian substance: what persists is not the ghost, but the conviction that a complete physical inventory would leave something out.

Ryle's dissolution: the category mistake

Gilbert Ryle's The Concept of Mind (1949) attacked the "ghost in the machine" as a category mistake — treating dispositional and capacity talk as if it named a second, hidden theatre. Ryle's behaviorism is now generally judged too strong, but his diagnostic survives: much soul-talk is talk about the person's abilities and proclivities, mistaken for talk about an inner object.

"It would be easier for me to concede matter and extension to the soul than to concede the capacity to move a body and be moved by it to an immaterial thing." — Elisabeth of Bohemia to Descartes, 10 June 1643

III · The forensic soul of the Abrahamic theologies

In the Jewish, Christian, and Islamic traditions the soul's center of gravity is neither animation nor metaphysics but accountability. The soul is that in the person which stands before God, receives judgment, and bears desert; its persistence beyond death is required not primarily to explain life but to guarantee that the moral ledger closes. This forensic orientation explains features of the theological soul that puzzle purely philosophical readers: its individuation (each person's account must be separately kept), its indestructibility or divine preservation (the ledger must not be lost), and the intensity of the disputes over its origin (when the accountable subject begins).

The internal disagreements are substantial and instructive. The Hebrew scriptures speak of nefesh (the living being itself), ruaḥ (breath, spirit), and neshamah (the divine in-breathing) without a systematic doctrine of an immortal detachable soul; later rabbinic and medieval thought developed such doctrines in conversation with Greek philosophy. Christian theology divided over whether the soul sleeps until resurrection or proceeds immediately to its state; Islamic kalām debated whether the nafs is a subtle body or an immaterial substance. Across all three, bodily resurrection — not disembodied survival — remains the orthodox terminus, a fact that separates the theological soul sharply from its Platonic ancestor.

Origin disputes: traducianism vs. creationism

Whether each soul is generated by the parents with the body (traducianism) or created directly by God at each conception (creationism) divided Latin theology for centuries. The dispute is forensically motivated: traducianism eases the transmission of original sin but risks making the soul material; creationism protects the soul's dignity but makes God the author of a corrupted subject. The pattern — metaphysics adjusted to fit moral bookkeeping — is characteristic of the entire register.

Aquinas' synthesis: subsistent form

Aquinas (Summa Theologiae I, qq. 75–76) fused Aristotle's hylomorphism with the forensic requirements: the soul is the form of the body, yet a subsistent form capable of surviving separation — though in a condition so truncated that Aquinas insists the separated soul is not the person, and full personhood awaits reunion with the body. The synthesis is philosophically strained and theologically exact, which is precisely the point: the doctrine's shape is dictated by the work it must do.

Al-Ghazālī and the philosophers

Al-Ghazālī's critique of the Aristotelian philosophers turned partly on the resurrection: a philosophy that can guarantee only the soul's survival, and not the body's return, fails the tradition's forensic specification. His willingness to subordinate the era's best metaphysics to that specification again illustrates the priority of the moral function in this family of concepts.

COMPARATIVE FINDING

The theological soul is the only major formulation in which the moral function dominates the other three. Its successors in secular ethics — the rational agent, the person as end-in-itself, the bearer of rights — inherit the accountability role while abandoning the metaphysical guarantee that the account will be settled. Whether moral concepts calibrated for a closed ledger remain stable on an open one is treated in Section VII.

IV · Dharmic analyses: the essential self and its denial

The Upanishadic tradition advances the strongest identity-function claim in the comparative record: beneath the empirical self of memory, character, and social role lies ātman — unborn, unchanging, and in the non-dualist reading identical with brahman, the ground of reality as such. The self is not merely immortal; it is, at the limit, the only thing that was never in danger. Liberation (mokṣa) consists in the dissolution of the misidentification of ātman with its psychophysical wrappings.

Early Buddhism, emerging from the same milieu and sharing its soteriological grammar, denies the load-bearing posit. The anattā analysis decomposes the person into five aggregates (skandhas) — form, feeling, perception, formations, consciousness — each impermanent, none a self, their continuity requiring no underlying substrate, as a flame passes from wick to wick. What we call a person is a designation applied to a causally connected stream; the sense of an unchanging inner owner is itself a cognitive construction, and its deconstruction is the point of the path.

The chariot argument (Milindapañha)

The monk Nāgasena's reply to King Milinda remains the tradition's canonical illustration: as "chariot" is a designation for wheels, axle, and frame in assembly — with no chariot found among or beyond the parts — so "Nāgasena" designates the aggregates in process. The argument's force is that it concedes everything the essentialist wants to explain (reference, continuity, practical identity) while denying that a further entity is needed to explain it.

Karma without a carrier?

The standing objection to anattā is forensic: if no self persists, what carries karmic desert across lives? The tradition's answer — causal continuity without identity, as milk becomes curd — effectively decouples the moral function from the identity function, a separation no Western framework attempted before Parfit's Reasons and Persons (1984) reached a structurally similar position by independent argument.

Later syntheses and qualified selves

The Indian debate did not terminate in a binary. Vedāntic sub-schools graded the self's identity with brahman from strict non-dualism to qualified and dualist readings; Buddhist Pudgalavādins defended an "inexpressible" person neither identical to nor distinct from the aggregates. The persistence of these intermediate positions suggests that both the essentialist and eliminativist poles leave genuine explananda unclaimed.

"Just as, with an assemblage of parts, the word 'chariot' is used, so, when the aggregates exist, there is the convention 'a being.'" — Saṃyutta Nikāya V (Vajirā Sutta)

V · The mind sciences and the successor vocabulary

Scientific psychology formally retired "soul" within a generation of its founding. James' Principles of Psychology (1890) still discusses the soul-theory respectfully before setting it aside as explanatorily idle; by the behaviorist consolidation it was not even worth refuting. The functions, however, were not retired — they were reassigned. Animation went to physiology and thermodynamics; identity to memory, personality, and narrative-self research; the moral function to developmental and social psychology of agency; and the remainder function, officially, to nothing.

The contemporary picture is aggressively constructional. Memory is reconstructive rather than archival; the unified visual world is assembled from saccadic fragments; the sense of body-ownership can be displaced onto a rubber hand in minutes; split-brain preparations suggest that unity of consciousness is a default setting rather than a metaphysical guarantee. Self-model theories draw the natural conclusion: the self is the content of a transparent model the organism runs of itself — indispensable, real as a process, and not a thing.

What the reconstructive turn actually shows

The experimental dissolution of the self's apparent unities licenses a precise conclusion: the felt simplicity of the subject is not evidence of a simple substance. It does not license the stronger conclusion that there is no subject — a process can be real, causally potent, and worth the name. The slide from "the self is constructed" to "the self is an illusion" repeats, with inverted sign, the substantialist's original error of reading phenomenology as ontology.

Neural correlates and the explanatory gap

Correlational neuroscience can now track self-referential processing, autobiographical retrieval, and affective valuation with considerable anatomical specificity. What no correlation supplies is the entailment from physical description to experiential character — the gap property dualists inherit from Elisabeth's question. The mind sciences have not closed the remainder function; they have quarantined it.

Predictive processing and the self as hypothesis

On predictive-processing accounts, the organism maintains a generative model whose deepest hidden variable is itself; selfhood is the brain's best standing hypothesis about the source of its own regularities. The framework is notable here for reproducing, in computational dress, Aristotle's structural insight: the "soul" is not an occupant of the system but the system's own organization, described at the level at which it maintains itself.

Damasio's layered self

Damasio's distinction between proto-self, core self, and autobiographical self (1999) stratifies the identity function developmentally: a homeostatic body-mapping layer, a moment-to-moment subject of experience, and a narrative structure extended in time. The stratification matters comparatively because different traditions' soul-concepts map onto different layers — the Homeric psychē to the first, the Cartesian cogito to the second, the forensic soul to the third.

SECTION FINDING

The successor vocabulary performs the animation and identity functions to a high standard, the moral function partially, and the remainder function not at all — by design, since a discipline constituted by third-person method cannot adopt a task defined as resisting third-person description. The vacancy is structural, which is why it does not close as data accumulate.

VI · Social dimensions: personhood as conferred status

A final register treats personhood not as discovered within the individual but as conferred by the group. Legal systems have withheld full personhood from human beings and extended it to corporations, ships, rivers, and idols; ritual systems mark the entry into and exit from personhood with more ceremony than biology alone would warrant. In this register the operative question is not "what is a soul?" but "whom do we count, and for what?" — and the answers are visibly historical, contested, and revisable.

Durkheim's analysis of collective effervescence supplies the connecting mechanism: the experienced sacredness of the person is, on his account, the group's own force represented in individual form — the soul as society's deposit in each member. One need not accept the full reduction to notice what it explains: why soul-language intensifies precisely at the points of social incorporation and loss (birth, naming, marriage, death), and why desecration of the person is universally treated as an offense against more than the individual.

Personhood before the law

The legal person is the forensic soul secularized: a bookkeeping subject to which acts, debts, and rights are imputed. Its detachment from biology is complete — corporations hold rights of speech; some jurisdictions have granted legal personhood to rivers and ecosystems. The construct's portability confirms the present survey's functional thesis: where a function is clearly specified, it can be reassigned to a new bearer without metaphysical residue.

The soul of a group

Everyday speech ascribes soul to bands, teams, neighborhoods, and nations, and its loss is reported with the same vocabulary of hollowing used for persons. The usage is not decorative: it tracks the presence or absence of the integrative processes — shared history, mutual recognition, self-maintenance — that the Aristotelian analysis treats as soul in the primary sense. Group soul-talk is hylomorphism applied at scale.

Digital afterlives and the distributed person

Posthumous social-media profiles, trained conversational replicas, and memorial archives now perform identity-function work for the dead — maintaining address, voice, and social presence without animation. The phenomenon splits functions that every prior tradition bundled, and public unease about it is best read as the felt seam of that split: the remainder function objecting to its own automation.

"The soul is society individualized — the sacred within us is the group's own force, represented." — after Durkheim, The Elementary Forms of the Religious Life (1912)

VII · Discussion: four functions, one vacancy

Read functionally, the comparative record resolves into four families of soul-concepts, each organized around the function it performs best. The substance family (Plato, Descartes, the kalām) secures identity and remainder at the cost of the interface problem. The form family (Aristotle, Aquinas in part, modern functionalism) secures animation and identity at the cost of survival. The process family (Buddhist anattā, Humean bundles, self-model theory) dissolves the bearer while preserving the phenomena. The status family (forensic theology, legal personhood, Durkheimian analysis) makes personhood a conferred standing and thereby explains its social behavior. No family performs all four functions; each family's characteristic pathology is the function it neglects.

The persistence of the word

The survey's terminal observation is lexical. Populations that assent to no doctrine of the soul continue to reach for the word at predictable points: for depth in persons ("an old soul"), for expressive authenticity in art ("the record has soul"), for integrative wholeness in groups and places, and for the specific loss felt when a person's body persists while the person seems absent. These usages cluster exactly over the remainder function — the aspects of personhood that resist inventory. On the present analysis this is not nostalgia but precision: the word survives because it is still the only single term whose job description matches the vacancy. Concepts are retired when their work is reassigned; "soul" remains employed because part of its work has nowhere else to go.

We accordingly end not with adjudication but with a conservation claim: across twenty-six centuries the explanatory mass of the soul-concept is neither created nor destroyed — it is redistributed. Every framework surveyed here, including the most austere, keeps a place-holder where the soul used to stand. The interesting question, and the subject of the sequel monograph, is what it costs a framework to leave that position unfilled.
